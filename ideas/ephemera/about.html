Every film here comes from the Prelinger Archives, a collection of ephemeral film held at the Internet Archive. This site is a browser on top of that collection, nothing more: nothing is hosted here. Press play on a film and it streams straight from archive.org, on their bandwidth, and the download button on each film goes there too.

The catalogue is drawn from the Prelinger collection with home movies filtered out. Home movies exist there in their own right, but they are a different kind of thing from the industrial and institutional films this site is about.

What is ephemeral film?

Ephemeral film is anything made for a purpose other than art or entertainment: industrial process films, classroom filmstrips, government civil-defense and public-health reels, sales pitches, safety demonstrations, corporate training. It was made to do a job once and then be forgotten — the name comes from the fact that almost none of it was meant to last. What survives is a strange, specific record of how institutions in the 20th century tried to instruct, sell to and reassure people, on film that mostly nobody expected anyone to keep.

What's here, honestly

The catalogue's metadata is uneven, because it is uneven at the source. Archive.org's records for these films were entered over decades by different people with different amounts of time, so some films arrive with a full description and a clear date, and plenty arrive with neither.

The length figure is high because it isn't metadata anyone had to type — it's read off the video file itself. The 9% with no length are items whose file durations Archive.org doesn't report. The length slider hides those films while it's narrowed, since there's no honest way to place a film with no known length inside a range; widen the slider back to its full span and they return.

The tag figure is why the chips along the top of the browse view should be read as entry points into the collection, not as a complete index of it — 41 chips cover a topic well enough to be worth a filter, but most films don't carry any of those 41 subjects. Search reaches further, since it matches against titles and descriptions as well as tags, so if a chip doesn't turn up what you're looking for, searching for it is worth trying next.

Why some films have no year

Archive.org's date field doesn't always hold the year a film was made. Sometimes it holds the day the reel was digitized and uploaded instead — which, taken at face value, would make a 1930s industrial film look like it was shot decades from now. Rather than show a wrong date as if it were real, any date that resolves to later than 2015 is dropped and the film is shown with no year at all. That's a deliberate trade: a missing year is honest, a digitization date dressed up as a film date is not.

Public domain

These films are believed to be in the public domain or otherwise free of known copyright restrictions — that determination comes from the Prelinger Archives and the Internet Archive, not from this site. It's why downloading a film is offered directly from its detail panel rather than only linking out to watch it. Public-domain status can still vary by country, and a small number of items are genuinely disputed; nothing here is legal advice.
